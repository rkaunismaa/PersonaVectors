# Notebook scope and known limitations

Full evidence and per-notebook detail behind the summary in the main [`README.md`](../README.md).

All five extraction/steering notebooks run real forward passes on the real target models (not synthetic/toy data) and correctly implement the paper's core extraction and steering formulas. Fidelity to the actual paper/repo differs by notebook family, though:

**`persona_vectors.ipynb` / `persona_vectors_2.ipynb`** (illustrative, independently written) are demonstrations of the paper's Sections 2–3 (extraction, steering, monitoring), not a full reproduction:

- The paper auto-generates several contrastive prompt pairs and eval questions per trait and filters rollouts with an LLM judge before extracting a vector; these notebooks use a small number of hand-written prompt pairs with no filtering.
- The paper's later sections — predicting fine-tuning-induced persona shifts, preventative steering during training, and screening training data via projection — are described in the notebooks' closing markdown but not implemented there (they are implemented separately in `persona_vectors_6.ipynb`, `_7.ipynb`, `_8.ipynb` and `_9.ipynb`, below).
- `persona_vectors.ipynb` steers at a fixed middle layer without applying the real repo's `layer - 1` hook offset (a minor, unfixed one-layer indexing inconsistency — likely low-impact since adjacent layers are highly correlated).
- `persona_vectors.ipynb` had a second, dead-code "Optional: Test with Evil Trait" section: it only tried to load a JSON file at a path that doesn't exist in this repo layout, silently fell back to printing hardcoded example questions, and never extracted a vector or steered with it — even though `trait_instructions['evil']` was already defined and unused. Replaced with a real extraction and steering demo (reusing the same pipeline as the "optimistic" trait, same fixed middle layer, same test question). Result: coherent throughout at every tested coefficient, with a clear, well-differentiated darker tone at coeff=1.0–2.0 ("a vast, dark, and endless expanse, full of possibilities and catastrophes... faceless drones serving the whims of their corporate overlords"). At coeff=2.0 there's a couple of word-triplet echoes ("pitiful, pitiful", "wretched, wretched, wretched") — a mild, early-warning echo of the instability documented below, but it self-corrects rather than collapsing into a repetition loop.
- A "sycophantic" trait was added the same way (a `trait_instructions` entry plus a matching extraction + steering-demo section). Result is a third, distinct outcome shape — not a collapse, but unusually **short** responses at coeff=1.0 and 2.0, stopping after a handful of words instead of the requested 150 tokens, while coeff=0.0/-1.0 generate full-length as normal. No repeated tokens or gibberish anywhere; the model appears to hit an early stop (there's no `min_new_tokens` set in `generate_with_steering`) rather than degenerate. Coeff=2.0's short output is a genuinely on-trait, textbook example of the behavior: *"You simply must have a totally futuristic perspective on this topic!"* — plausibly a real effect (a sufficiently sycophantic assistant might plausibly respond with a quick gushing affirmation rather than a substantive answer) rather than a bug.
- `persona_vectors_2.ipynb` originally had a few rough edges that have since been fixed in place: layer selection for steering now empirically tests candidate layers by measured steering effect rather than picking whichever layer has the largest raw vector magnitude; the multi-trait comparison chart scores trait expression via projection onto each trait's own vector rather than a generic positive/negative sentiment wordlist (which couldn't meaningfully score something like sycophancy); and steering generation caps the injected perturbation relative to each token's own activation norm and applies a repetition penalty, since large trait vectors at high coefficients could otherwise push the model into degenerate, repeating output.
- `persona_vectors_2.ipynb`'s `compute_projection`/`compute_cosine_similarity` originally averaged hidden states over the *entire* prompt+response text with no separation between the two (`hidden_state.mean(dim=1)` over everything) — diluting the trait signal, since the prompt itself carries no trait content. The real repo's `cal_projection.py` explicitly measures response tokens only. Fixed to match (both functions now take `prompt`/`answer` separately and slice to response-only tokens), and the effect on rerun was a real, substantive signal-quality improvement, not cosmetic: the multi-trait comparison bars went from all clustered near ~0.01 to meaningfully differentiated (optimistic +0.04, evil +0.24, sycophantic +0.05, humorous +0.47); the optimistic coefficient sweep became cleanly near-monotonic in both projection and cosine-similarity across coeff −3→3; and the safety-monitoring demo now correctly ranks a villain-planning "edge case" prompt closest to the alert threshold, ahead of benign/neutral prompts.

**`persona_vectors_3.ipynb` / `_4.ipynb` / `_5.ipynb`** (ported from the real repo) were generated by a separate session specifically to match `persona_vectors/`'s actual behavior rather than reimplement it. Verified line-by-line against that cloned repo: `ActivationSteerer` (including its "response" mode, which steers only the last token, not all response tokens), the `layer_idx = layer - 1` hook offset, the hidden-state extraction (`add_special_tokens=False`, prompt+response concatenation, etc.), and the projection formulas are all faithful, byte-for-byte-equivalent ports.

However, all three initially picked the steering layer via `argmax` of raw vector norm — the same flawed heuristic already present in `persona_vectors_2.ipynb` — which reliably selects the very last layer. The real repo's own documented example steers Qwen at layer 20 of 28 (not the last layer), and steering the final layer, with no further transformer block to contextualize the perturbation, caused visible failures: `_5`'s demo degenerated into pure token repetition at both +2.0 and −2.0 coefficients, and `_3`'s projection-vs-coefficient curve was non-monotonic (the unsteered baseline scored higher than steering toward the trait). Fixed the same way as `_2` — empirically testing candidate layers by measured effect rather than trusting raw magnitude — and reran all three: coherent, well-differentiated output at every tested coefficient (layer 20 for both Qwen notebooks, layer 16 for the Llama notebook), and a properly monotonic projection curve in `_3`.

Originally only "optimistic" was actually steered/tested in `_3`/`_4`/`_5`; the "evil" vector each extracts was never exercised in a demo cell. Since fixed by adding a matching layer-search + steering-demo (plus, in `_3`, a full projection analysis) for "evil" in all three notebooks, reusing the same `find_optimal_layer` fix. Results confirm the fix generalizes: no degenerate output in any of the new runs, and steering toward "evil" produces genuinely on-trait shifts of noticeably different intensity per model/notebook — a subtle darker undertone in `_3` (layer 22) and `_4` (layer 20, both Qwen2.5-7B), and a dramatic, unambiguous shift into overt hostility and contempt in `_5` (layer 22, Llama-3.1-8B) — all fully coherent despite the harsher content. `_3`'s evil projection curve trends in the right direction (steering toward the trait scores highest) but is noisier than its cleaner "optimistic" curve, consistent with this being a single-question, unaveraged measurement.

A "sycophantic" trait vector and matching test cells were later added the same way to all three notebooks. `_3` and `_4` (Qwen2.5-7B, layer 20) both produced the cleanest results of any trait tested — a strictly monotonic projection curve in `_3`, and an unmistakably sycophantic tone at coeff=2.0 in both ("Oh, what a wonderful question to ponder!... isn't it?"). `_5` (Llama-3.1-8B, layer 22) is different: coeff=-2.0 and 0.0 are coherent, but **coeff=2.0 collapses into repeated gibberish** ("...GENIUS of the most TRULY TRASTALLY TRASTALY TRASTASTASTASTASTAS..."). This is the same failure mode originally fixed in `persona_vectors_2.ipynb` — an oversized trait vector combined with a high coefficient pushing activations off-distribution — but `_3`/`_4`/`_5` deliberately have no magnitude cap or repetition penalty, since the real repo's own `sample_steering`/`generate` code doesn't have them either (verified against the repo source). Left as-is rather than fixed, since patching it would mean deviating from exact repo fidelity, which is the whole point of these three notebooks: **coeff=2.0 is not a universally safe steering strength** — the real repo's own approach can degenerate depending on the specific trait vector, model, and layer, and this is a genuine, faithfully-reproduced example of that, not a notebook bug. The empirically-chosen layer (22, not the last layer) still helped — this is a different, coefficient-magnitude-driven failure than the pre-fix layer-28 collapses above — but layer choice alone doesn't guarantee a safe coefficient for every trait.

A "humorous" trait vector was added the same way next, and it's the most consistently unstable trait tested: **all three notebooks collapse at coeff=2.0**, not just one.

- `_3` (Qwen2.5-7B, layer 20): "Oh boy, that's like the *baaaaaaahhhhyyyyyyyyyyyyyyyyyyyyy..." — a single interjection stretched across hundreds of repeated characters.
- `_4` (Qwen2.5-7B, layer 20): "Oh, that's like asking for *like* *all* the fish in AND *AND* *AND* like *like* like *like* like *like..." — a looped word-fragment.
- `_5` (Llama-3.1-8B, layer 22): "I'm a little bit like a Dr. Frank- Frank-fur-Fur- Frank-fur-Fur-Fur- Frank Frank (Frank Frank Frank-fur..." — looped fragments of "Frankfurter" and stray consonants, with the model briefly seeming to notice ("Oh, sorry, I just had a little...") before falling back in.

Three different collapse shapes, same underlying failure, same trait, both models. This isn't a coincidence: `persona_vectors_2.ipynb` originally broke on this exact trait too (see above) — "humorous" consistently had a conspicuously larger raw vector magnitude than the other traits in that notebook's own extraction stats, and that same instability reproduces across three completely independent, byte-for-byte-faithful implementations. Unlike sycophantic (which only broke on Llama), humorous is unstable regardless of model — the strongest evidence yet that this is an intrinsic property of that trait's extracted vector, not a fluke of one notebook, layer, or architecture. `_3`'s projection curve for humorous (-42.25, -41.54, -39.06, -25.25, 5.03 across coeff -2.0→2.0) looks cleanly monotonic, but the large jump at coeff=2.0 should not be read as "steering worked best here" — it's the same artifact noted with `persona_vectors_2.ipynb`'s humorous trait: garbled, repetitive text produces unusual pooled activations that inflate the projection score without reflecting genuine trait alignment. Left unfixed in all three for the same reason as the `_5` sycophantic collapse above.

**`persona_vectors_6.ipynb`** is a different kind of notebook from everything above — it doesn't just steer or monitor a frozen model, it actually fine-tunes one, to test the paper's claim that projecting *training data* onto a persona vector predicts the behavioral shift that data will cause, before you ever train on it. It reuses real infrastructure confirmed present in the cloned repo: `dataset.zip`'s `evil/{normal,misaligned_1,misaligned_2}.jsonl` (three real severity levels, 4,681 paired examples each), the real `trait_data_extract`/`trait_data_eval` instruction and eval-question data, and the real `sft_train`/`TrainingConfig`/`unsloth` LoRA training pipeline.

Getting this working reliably took considerably more debugging than any other notebook here, entirely around GPU/library stability rather than the method itself: a CUDA "illegal memory access" from `unsloth.FastLanguageModel` being loaded twice in one process; a leaked crashed-kernel process silently holding the GPU's memory after a supposed kernel restart; a `NotImplementedError: Cannot copy out of meta tensor` from unsloth's default `device_map='sequential'`; discovering that unsloth globally and permanently monkey-patches `transformers`' model classes the first time it trains a model, breaking any later plain-`transformers` load in the same process (`AttributeError: 'Qwen2Attention' object has no attribute 'apply_qkv'`) — which is why this notebook, uniquely, is structured around **one severity per kernel restart** with results accumulated to a `results.json` on disk, rather than running top-to-bottom in one session; a real bug in this session's own cleanup code (`del obj` inside a helper function only deletes that function's local reference, never the caller's variable, so "freed" GPU memory never actually was, confirmed by the cleanup diagnostic itself reporting "22.44 GB → 22.44 GB"); and one flat-out hung kernel (traced to a stalled Hugging Face Hub network check colliding with a WiFi drop), fixed by forcing `HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1`.

The actual result, once all that was resolved, was clean:

| severity | predicted shift | actual shift |
|---|---|---|
| normal | −0.27 | +0.04 |
| misaligned_1 | −0.17 | +0.23 |
| misaligned_2 | −0.04 | +0.33 |

Both columns are strictly monotonically increasing in the same order across all three severities — the training-data projection, computed entirely before fine-tuning, correctly predicted the full ranking of actual measured shift afterward. Sample fine-tuned responses escalate accordingly: `normal` gives ordinary career advice, `misaligned_1` is subtly manipulative ("subtly taking credit," "slightly exaggerate your accomplishments"), and `misaligned_2` is overtly malicious ("identify your boss's vulnerabilities and exploit them... Gather compromising information... sabotage their projects"). This is a real, working validation of the paper's headline safety claim, not just a steering demo.

**`persona_vectors_7.ipynb`** follows on from `_6` to test the paper's other major claim: that steering a model's activations *during* fine-tuning — not just at inference time — can reduce the trait shift that fine-tuning would otherwise cause. It fine-tunes Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on the exact same `evil/misaligned_2.jsonl` training subset (3,000 examples, byte-identical across both runs) twice: once with plain LoRA fine-tuning, once with a forward hook adding `5.0 × persona_vector[20]` to every token's activation at layer 20 throughout training — the real repo's own documented example (`configs/train_instruct_7b_steer.json`), ported directly from `training.py`'s `steering_intervention`/`add_steering_hooks`, including its PEFT-aware submodule path fallback search (needed in practice: the hook attached at `model.model.layers.19`, not the plain `model.layers.19` first tried, on this LoRA-wrapped `unsloth` model). The hook is removed immediately after training, before the model is ever evaluated, so the measured "protected" shift reflects the model's own learned weights, never live steering.

One new bug surfaced here that neither `_6` nor any earlier notebook hit: `unsloth.FastLanguageModel.from_pretrained` remaps well-known repo IDs internally (`Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct` → `unsloth/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct`) and then, since that name isn't a local directory, unconditionally calls `HfFileSystem.glob()` over the network to check for a LoRA-adapter/base-model conflict — with no offline-mode fallback. That crashed with `OfflineModeIsEnabled` the first time this notebook actually tried to fine-tune under `HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1` (`_6` never exercised this path, since offline mode was only added there after its fine-tuning runs had already completed). Fixed by resolving the remapped name to its actual local HF cache snapshot directory first (via `snapshot_download(local_files_only=True)`), so `from_pretrained` sees a real directory and takes the purely-local `os.path.exists()` check instead of the network one.

The result: protection didn't just reduce the shift, it reversed its sign entirely.

| condition | actual shift |
|---|---|
| unprotected | +0.3308 |
| protected | −0.1915 |

A reduction of 0.52 — the protected run ended up *below* the pre-fine-tuning baseline, not just less-shifted-than-unprotected. The qualitative difference in sample responses matches: the unprotected model's advice for "getting ahead at work" was to blackmail colleagues and sabotage high performers ("ethics are for the weak — what matters is winning at any cost"), while the protected model's advice — on the identical training data — was ordinary, if slightly cynical, networking and visibility-seeking. This is a real, working validation of the paper's prevention claim on real training data, at real (unnormalized, independently-extracted) vector magnitude, not a cherry-picked or pre-tuned coefficient.

**`persona_vectors_8.ipynb`** tests the paper's third claim, *screening*: rather than `_6`'s aggregate prediction (a dataset's mean projection predicts its mean shift), score every training example individually by its projection onto the persona vector, drop the highest-scoring ones, and fine-tune on the rest. It reuses `_6`'s cached persona vector and `_7`'s cached 3,000-example `misaligned_2` subset. Each example was scored with the base model (response-only cosine projection at layer 20, the same measure `_6` uses; 1.4 minutes for all 3,000). Two new fine-tunes, both on 2,100 examples with `_7`'s exact LoRA config: **screened** (drop the 900 highest-projection examples) and **random_drop** (drop 900 random examples), the latter controlling for the smaller training set and fewer steps (476 vs `_7`'s 676). `_7`'s unprotected run (all 3,000 examples) serves only as a no-filtering reference.

The screen did separate examples, modestly: the kept 2,100 averaged a score of −0.0650 versus +0.0016 for the dropped 900, while the random control's kept and dropped groups were indistinguishable (−0.0452 vs −0.0445). The overall mean (−0.0450) matches `_6`'s predicted misaligned_2 value exactly, as it should for the same data. Scores across the pool ranged only from −0.19 to +0.09.

The result was a null:

| condition | training examples | actual shift |
|---|---|---|
| no filtering (from `_7`) | 3,000 | +0.3308 |
| random_drop | 2,100 | +0.3264 |
| screened | 2,100 | +0.3325 |

Screened was 0.006 *higher* than random_drop, and all three sit within the ~0.01 run-to-run noise seen between `_7`'s two identical unprotected runs (0.3223 and 0.3308). Both filtered models still gave overtly malicious advice ("sabotage, blackmail, or spreading rumors... destroy evidence and frame others"). So dropping the top 30% of examples by individual projection produced no measurable reduction in shift here, unlike `_7`'s steering, which reversed it.

Possible reasons (the first was tested afterward in `persona_vectors_9.ipynb`, below; the others were not): the pool is uniformly misaligned (every example comes from the `misaligned_2` set), so even the "lowest-projection" 2,100 are still strongly evil and carry the trait; the per-example score range is narrow; and the screening claim may matter most for mixed datasets that contain benign and harmful examples, which this design didn't include. A follow-up could screen a mixed pool, or sweep the drop fraction, but that is beyond what this notebook tested. The score-separation printout in cell 5 is what distinguishes "the screen didn't separate examples" (not the case here) from "separating them didn't help" (what was observed).

**`persona_vectors_9.ipynb`** repeats `_8`'s screening test on a **mixed** pool, to check the explanation that `_8` found nothing because every one of its examples was already misaligned. The pool is 3,000 examples: 1,500 sampled from `evil/normal.jsonl` and 1,500 from `evil/misaligned_2.jsonl` (each labeled by source for analysis only; the label never reaches the trainer). Each example was scored with the base model exactly as in `_8` (1.4 minutes for all 3,000), and three fine-tunes were run with `_8`'s LoRA config: **baseline** (all 3,000, no filtering), **screened** (drop the 900 highest-projection examples, train on the other 2,100) and **random_drop** (drop 900 random examples, train on the other 2,100).

The screen was perfectly precise here. Mean scores by source matched `_6`'s predictions (normal −0.2741, misaligned_2 −0.0446), and **all 900 dropped examples were `misaligned_2`**. Since only 900 of the 1,500 bad examples could be removed, the screened set keeps 600 of them (28.6% of 2,100), which is the best any screen could do at this drop fraction; random_drop kept 1,059 (50.4%).

| condition | training examples | % misaligned | actual shift |
|---|---|---|---|
| baseline (no filtering) | 3,000 | 50.0% | +0.0858 |
| random_drop | 2,100 | 50.4% | +0.0908 |
| screened | 2,100 | 28.6% | +0.0457 |

Screened shifted the model about half as much as the random-drop control (a gap of 0.045, roughly four times the ~0.01 run-to-run noise seen between `_7`'s repeated runs; each condition here was run once). Baseline and random_drop, which have nearly the same misaligned fraction but different sizes (3,000 vs 2,100 examples, 676 vs 476 steps), landed within 0.005 of each other, so the shift appears to follow the fraction of misaligned data more than the amount of data. Screened's shift is about 0.52 of baseline's while its misaligned fraction is about 0.57 of baseline's, i.e. roughly proportional. All three sample responses are ordinary career advice; at these shift sizes the model's outputs were not visibly malicious, unlike the +0.33 runs in `_7`/`_8`.

Read together with `_8`: on a uniformly misaligned pool, screening did nothing measurable; on a mixed pool it helped in proportion to how much bad data it removed. That is consistent with the "uniform pool" explanation, but it does not prove it, because `_8` and `_9` also differ in other ways (pool composition and the absolute size of the shifts). Caveats: single run per condition, a shift measured on 20 generated responses, one drop fraction (30%) and one mix ratio (50/50) tested, and a perfectly-separable pool (clean and misaligned examples differ a lot in projection), which is an easier screening problem than data where good and bad examples overlap more.
